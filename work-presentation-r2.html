Bebop

@ Microsoft · M365 Copilot · 2025–2026

How do you hold a quality bar when the medium hasn't settled yet?

context

Microsoft's redesign of M365 Copilot. The challenge wasn't visual polish — it was defining what "done" looked like for an AI response before there was a reference to point at. My work was about building that quality bar and making it defensible across a large team.

process
  • Leadership reviews as quality infrastructure Structured feedback loops that pressure-tested every decision — not just "does this look good" but "can we articulate why"
  • Multiple alternatives before any direction locked Ensuring the final decisions were chosen, not defaulted into — visible tradeoffs made the quality bar legible to the full team
  • Prototypes as quality artifacts Motion, timing, and response rhythm can only be evaluated experientially — prototypes were the spec, not a supplement to it
  • Cross-functional critique with engineering Understanding what was buildable helped clarify where it was worth pushing harder — not every quality bar costs the same to defend
decisions
  • What "finished" looks like for generative output Explicit criteria for when a response felt designed — not just rendered. Hierarchy, affordance placement, and visual rhythm as evaluable standards
  • Consistency as a system, not a checklist Patterns that flexed with content type while preserving the same intentionality — so the quality bar held even when the structure was unpredictable
  • The quality of details nobody notices Timing and transition details invisible to most users — but which would have felt wrong if they weren't there. That's the threshold for craft
outcome

shipped The work shipped as part of the M365 Copilot redesign featured in Microsoft's "Introducing a new design for Microsoft 365 Copilot" article, May 2026.

GenUI

@ Microsoft · M365 Copilot · 2025–2026

How do you build constraints that make creative exploration legible to a team?

context

Part of "Expressions" — an art direction initiative to define what Copilot output could look like beyond markdown. The brief was open: if the model can generate anything, what should it generate? The work was about turning that openness into something a team could evaluate, align on, and build toward.

process
  • Framing exploration for stakeholder alignment Open-ended briefs require a frame — not to constrain the work, but so reviewers can evaluate it rather than just react to it
  • Prototype as the primary design artifact No established patterns meant the prototype had to carry the weight specs normally carry — the only way to show what "structured output" could feel like
  • Wired real model responses into the layouts Not dummy content — actual model output shaped by compositional rules, so the exploration was grounded in what the system could actually produce
decisions
  • Compositional constraints as creative tools Tight rules for layout, hierarchy, and component structure that made dynamic output feel crafted — constraints that enabled creativity rather than bounded it
  • When structure overtakes prose Explicit thresholds — query complexity, information density, response length — so the format switch felt principled, not arbitrary. That's where systematic thinking lives
  • What "systematic" means in a generative context The system doesn't produce a template. It produces conditions under which any output feels designed — a harder and more interesting design problem than a fixed layout

Agent Builder

@ Microsoft · M365 Copilot · 2024–2025

What does designing in close partnership with engineering actually change?

context

Agent configuration had been a developer task. Making it native to a consumer product meant aligning design intent with engineering feasibility from the start — not pitching a vision and reconciling it with reality afterward.

process
  • Embedded with the Agent Builder engineering team Weekly syncs that kept design and engineering in active dialogue — not periodic handoffs where assumptions compound between reviews
  • Three flows explored in parallel as negotiation tools Not aesthetic alternatives — real bases for comparison that surfaced engineering tradeoffs early and kept the conversation concrete
  • Early design reviews before requirements compounded Sharing rough direction early meant feedback arrived at the moment it could still shape scope, not after the cost of change had already escalated
  • Guided user testing — ~15 participants Structured walkthroughs of all three flows; surfaced where users got stuck and which felt most natural — data that the engineering team could act on directly
decisions
  • In-flow creation over wizard UI Emerged from engineering partnership — understanding architectural constraints shaped what felt experientially right. The design decision and the engineering decision were the same decision
  • Progressive complexity without a cliff A low floor / high ceiling model that designers, engineers, and PMs could align on before a single pixel was final — a shared language for the experience
  • Making capability legible Trust signals that came directly from user testing — what participants believed, what they doubted. Collaboration meant those findings went straight back into engineering tradeoffs

Flubert

@ Microsoft · Fluent Design Studio · 2024–2025

What happens when you try to encode design quality into a system?

context

Design reviews don't scale. Maintaining visual consistency across thousands of Fluent components meant individual reviewer judgment was the bottleneck. Flubert — a Figma plugin — was built to systematize that judgment: catching the high-frequency, low-ambiguity issues so reviewers could focus on the hard ones.

process
  • Close collaboration with the Fluent Design Studio team Understanding what quality looked like when codified — which standards could be evaluated programmatically and which required human judgment to protect
  • From proof-of-concept to production tool Iterating on what designers actually needed — not just technically capable, but integrated into the workflow at the moment decisions get made
  • Designer-in-the-loop by design The tool surfaces recommendations, not verdicts — a deliberate choice to augment reviewer judgment rather than replace it
decisions
  • Vector database for semantic asset retrieval Scraped Fluent icon and emoji metadata to build a retrieval corpus — so suggestions are semantically relevant, not just string-matched. Quality at the retrieval layer
  • LLM for guideline evaluation Using a language model to evaluate UI against Fluent design guidelines — translating prose standards into evaluable criteria was itself a design problem
  • What to automate, what to protect Not every design judgment should be systematized. Flubert is intentionally narrow — the constraint was a principle, not a limitation

on quality

Great isn't a feeling — it's a set of decisions you can articulate. It's the timing detail nobody notices until it's wrong. It's the constraint framework that makes generative output feel intentional. It's the threshold for when to automate judgment and when to protect it.

The best work from these projects came from close collaboration — not just proximity, but real dialogue. Feedback that arrived before assumptions compounded. Engineering constraints that clarified what was worth fighting for. User testing that went straight back into product decisions. That's what collaboration actually changes.
