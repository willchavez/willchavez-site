I was closer to dropping Human-Computer Interaction than I expected to be. The class asked for a lot: research, analysis, sketches, prototypes, testing, and another round of changes, all while the rest of life kept happening. I missed some plans with friends, and more than once I wondered if I had taken on too much.

My wife kept reminding me why I had started and coached me into staying. I’m glad she did. The class was intense, but it gave me a way to connect the parts of work I enjoy most: understanding people’s needs, making an idea tangible, and building the thing well enough that someone can react to it.

## Learning to see the problem

The course gave names to things I had felt in practice. Heuristics helped me look for specific usability problems instead of relying on “this feels off.” Cognitive load made me ask how much a person has to remember, scan, and keep in their head. The gulf of execution and the gulf of evaluation gave me a useful way to describe two different questions: *How do I do what I want?* and *How can I tell what just happened?*

The design lifecycle was just as useful as any one principle. Needfinding, alternatives, prototyping, evaluation, and iteration aren’t boxes to check before the real work starts. They help you decide what the real work should be. I saw that play out in two projects that started from very different frustrations.

## Making peer review easier to understand

For our group project, we looked at peer review in Canvas. The existing flow left students unsure where feedback belonged and whether it had actually been submitted. In our survey, 21 of 25 respondents said they were confused about where to write feedback, and 18 of 25 were unsure about submission status.

Our team explored a few directions. The concept I led was PeerView: a split-screen review where the paper stays beside its feedback, with comments attached to highlighted passages and grouped as a thought, question, nit, or action. Keeping the writing and the conversation together was a practical way to lower the amount a reviewer had to remember while moving between parts of the task.

## Giving subscriptions some structure

For my individual project, I turned to a problem I knew from being a YouTube user: a long list of subscriptions that was hard to browse and easy to abandon. I started by asking people about the task and evaluating the current experience, then sketched three alternatives. A folder-based sidebar came out ahead in the first round, so I combined that structure with scoped search and filters in the next prototype.

In the final survey, 25 people tried the design. Ratings were strongest for finding channels, understanding folders, and feeling in control. That didn’t mean every interaction was solved; moving channels between folders still needed work. It did give me a grounded next step instead of a guess about what to polish.

## What I’m taking with me

These projects reaffirmed that I want to work where design and engineering meet. I like turning research into an interaction, then getting close enough to the implementation to understand what the interaction really asks of a person. A heuristic is useful when it changes a decision; a prototype is useful when it makes a question concrete enough to test.

I also learned something less academic: I can care about doing good work without treating every assignment like a referendum on whether I belong here. I missed friend commitments, got stressed, and had to let some things be good enough. “B’s get degrees” became a helpful reminder that finishing the class and learning from it mattered more than squeezing a perfect result out of every week.

If you’re starting again at school or taking on a hard thing alongside an already full life, you’re allowed to make tradeoffs. Ask for support early. Keep the parts that matter, let some things be less than perfect, and give yourself credit for continuing. I’m grateful I did.
